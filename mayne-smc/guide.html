Reading this guide

A visual companion to the Trader Mayne "Whiteboard Series." Every concept is drawn as schematic candles so you can recognise it on a real chart. The diagram colours match the Pine indicator in this folder, so what you see here is what prints on your TradingView chart.

bullish candle bearish candle bullish FVG bearish FVG bullish OB bearish OB equilibrium 50% swing / liquidity level

The five core ideas

  1. Think like the institution. You are the big player who must fill large orders. You can't fill size at market without moving price against yourself, so you engineer liquidity: drive price into resting orders, fill against them, then make the real move.
  2. Price moves from liquidity to liquidity. The market ping-pongs between buy-side (above) and sell-side (below) liquidity. If you can't point at where price is going and why, you don't have a setup.
  3. Top-down, always. Minimum two timeframes. The HTF gives bias / structure / points of interest; the LTF gives entries, stops, targets. Never trade off the LTF alone.
  4. Price is fractal (the Russian doll). Inside a weekly range is a daily range, inside that an hourly range. The best trades are where multiple timeframes align.
  5. Markets seek efficiency; asymmetry pays. Inefficient one-directional moves (gaps) get rebalanced; obvious stops get hunted. Trade for asymmetric reward-to-risk so the win rate you need stays low.

1 · Market structure the foundation

Everything is built on this. If you can't read structure cleanly, the rest falls apart. A swing is just three candles; a trend is a sequence of them.

Swing high — middle candle's high is the highest, lower candle each side.
Swing low — middle candle's low is the lowest, higher candle each side.

Mayne uses a 3-candle swing. Swings are visual — the skeleton of price. If a swing isn't obvious, it probably isn't significant.

Uptrend = higher highs (HH) + higher lows (HL).
Downtrend = lower highs (LH) + lower lows (LL).
Range = no clear trend, often equal highs / equal lows. If you can't tell up or down, it's a range. Market structure = trend = direction = bias.

Break vs shift

MSB (continuation) — close through the prior high → assume the uptrend continues.
MSS (potential reversal) — first break against the trend (here, closing back above the last lower high).
Rules — MSB vs MSS
  • MSB = continuation. Uptrend: price closes through the prior higher high. Downtrend: closes through the prior lower low. Requires a close, not just a wick — a wick-through that closes back inside is a liquidity sweep, not a break.
  • MSS = potential reversal. Uptrend: price breaks a higher low. Downtrend: breaks a lower high. "Might" reverse — full reversal is confirmed only when structure then breaks the other way (MSS → LH → MSB down, or MSS → HL → MSB up).

Timeframe congruence — the key skill

A pullback on the HTF (forming a HL) can be a full downtrend on the LTF. The trade is the moment the LTF syncs back up: MSS then MSB in the HTF direction.

Trading the LTF in isolation gets you short right before the HTF reasserts and price rips. Wait for the re-sync.

2 · Liquidity why price moves

For this system, liquidity = resting orders — mostly retail stop losses plus breakout orders. They are the fuel smart money uses to fill positions.

BSL (buy-side liquidity) rests above — short stops + breakout longs; smart money sells into it. SSL rests below — long stops + breakdown shorts; smart money buys it.

Counter-intuition: to get long, price is often driven down first to trigger sells (SSL) the institution buys; to get short, driven up into BSL.

Break-and-close sweep — runs the level, closes back, then displaces.
SFP — a wick through a swing point that closes back inside, then displaces.
Rules — confirming a sweep

Two ingredients are mandatory, or it's a real breakout, not a sweep:

  1. Price runs through the level, then gets rejected (closes back on the original side).
  2. Displacement follows — a strong move, often causing an MSS the other way.

If price breaks and hovers without reclaiming, treat it as a genuine breakout. News (CPI, FOMC, earnings) is just the cover — "it was coming for that liquidity anyway."

ERL (swing high/low) = targets. IRL (minor swings, OBs, FVGs) = entries. Cycle: take ERL → target IRL → take IRL → target ERL.
Equal highs / lows — liquidity rests just beyond. Prime sweep targets.

A HTF sweep is significant and the move out of it should be powerful. A LTF counter-trend sweep against a strong HTF trend carries less weight — wait for the realign.

3 · Dealing range the working box

At any moment price sits inside a range, heading for liquidity above the high or below the low. The range tells you which is likelier, where to enter, and how far it can go.

Mark the significant swing high → swing low, draw the 50% (equilibrium). Discount below = look for longs. Premium above = look for shorts. "No diddle in the middle" — skip trades at equilibrium.
Rules — building & using the range
  • Mark the significant SH and SL (most recent + visually obvious; discretion allowed).
  • Draw the 50% with the Fib tool (low→high) so 0/50/100 auto-plot.
  • Discount = below 50% → buy low. Premium = above 50% → sell high. Equilibrium = no trade.
  • BSL sits above the high (external), SSL below the low (external).
  • Bullish range = swing low first then high; bearish = swing high first then low.
  • The range resets every MSB — each new leg becomes a new dealing range; redraw.
Nested ranges (Russian doll). Highest-probability long = hourly and daily and weekly all in discount. Zooming into the LTF range gives a far tighter stop and better RR than entering off the HTF.

IRL = entries, ERL = targets. Don't close at internal liquidity still inside the range, then watch price run to the real external target without you.

4 · High timeframe bias the master filter

The single most important filter — it answers one question: long or short? Establish it before looking for any entry. The highest timeframe is your boss; whatever it says goes.

The hierarchy (top-down)

TimeframeJob
WeeklyMacro direction
DailyMajor swings / where we are in the trend
H4Local trend (last few days–2 weeks)
H1 / M15Entry timeframes

If weekly + daily are bearish, the best M15 trade is a short, even amid a M15 uptrend. Bias proliferates top-down.

The two execution pairs Mayne names

Read bias on the higher chart, take the entry on the lower:

HTF (read bias)LTF (take the entry)Use when
H12H1Standard / swing pace
H4M15Faster, smaller setups

Don't jump Weekly straight to an hourly entry — step down through the hierarchy.

The 4-point checklist — run before EVERY trade

  1. HTF market structure — bull or bear? Where was the last MSB? What are weekly + daily doing?
  2. Dealing range — premium, discount, or equilibrium? (Longs only in discount; shorts only in premium; nothing at EQ.)
  3. Liquidity — what did we just take? What's the next pool, and does the RR to it make sense (asymmetric, ≥ 2:1)?
  4. Entry model — is there a clean POI (order block, breaker, or FVG)?

All four boxes or no trade. One fail (e.g. you're in premium not discount) = skip it, even if it would have worked. Re-establish bias on cadence — whenever the HTF shifts, start over.

5 · Entries: order blocks, breakers, FVGs points of interest

The actual entry zones. All three live in the same areas and the best setups overlap them. Context from §§1–4 is what separates a high- from low-probability POI — a POI in isolation is just a pattern.

Fair Value Gap (FVG)

Bullish FVG — gap from high of candle 1 to low of candle 3, left by a big green displacement candle. Price returns to fill it.
Bearish FVG — gap from low of candle 1 to high of candle 3, left by a big red displacement candle.
Rules — trading an FVG
  • Entry: the gap's 50% and into the zone — bullish: upper 50% (or the top edge); bearish: lower 50%. Ladder ("spray") across that half.
  • Stop: tight = just beyond the far edge of the gap (best RR); loose = beyond candle 1 of the pattern (fewer stop-outs, worse RR).
  • Target: external range liquidity on the opposite side.
  • Higher-probability when: in the correct half, formed after sweeping liquidity, aligned with HTF trend, bigger displacement candle. Not all FVGs fill, and not all that fill hold.

Order Block (OB)

Bullish OB — the last down candle before a strong up-move (ideally one that breaks structure). Demand zone; look for longs on retest.
Bearish OB — the last up candle before a strong down-move. Supply zone; look for shorts on retest.
Rules — trading an OB
  • Entry: from the open/top of the OB down to its 50% (mean threshold) for a bullish OB; bottom up to 50% for bearish. A strong OB should not close beyond its 50%.
  • Stop: just beyond the full OB (+ room for spread). Safer because a good OB already ran liquidity.
  • Target: external range liquidity (BSL above for longs, SSL below for shorts).
  • Higher-probability when: large displacement off it (leaves an FVG), the move breaks structure, it took liquidity first, bigger candle(s). OBs trade with the trend.

Breaker & the "unicorn" overlap

Breaker = a failed OB, i.e. an S/R flip. A failed bearish OB that price blows through becomes support → longs. Most common at reversals.
When OB + FVG (+ breaker) stack, that's the highest-probability POI in the system. Alarm bells — full attention.

OB vs FVG — which do you use? Ep8

A good OB creates an FVG, so they sit in the same area (OB just below, FVG just above in a bull leg). The recurring question: will price reverse at the FVG, or trade down to the OB? Mayne's honest answer is "it depends" — run five filters, and the last is the real decider.

  1. Location (premium/discount) — first and most important. Only POIs on the correct side; throws out most candidates.
  2. Context — aggressive trend → favour FVGs (shallow pullbacks); range → favour OBs; reversal → OBs + breakers.
  3. Quality — prefer the cleaner, more obvious POI. Tiny ambiguous candle = probably not the one.
  4. Risk-to-reward — if the FVG doesn't give ≥ 2R, wait for the deeper OB or accept you may miss the trade.
  5. Low timeframe — the actual answer. Zoom in. Whichever POI is where the LTF flips from downtrend to uptrend (MSS → MSB, re-syncing with the HTF) is the one that holds. If the OB also fails to flip, that's a likely trend change — stand aside.

Mayne's own practice: HTF POIs = OBs and breakers; LTF entries = breakers mainly. FVGs shine on micro timeframes (15m → 5m) in a clean trend. The real rule is use what works for you; obviousness is the tell.

6 · Frameworks

Power of Three (PO3) / AMD

Accumulation (sideways build-up) → Manipulation (the fake-out / stop-hunt the wrong way) → Distribution (the real move). Enter on manipulation, take profit in distribution.

Judas Swing

The manipulation arm of AMD — the fake move that betrays you. Often at the session start: price dips below the open to run SSL, then reverses and trends all day.

Kill Zones

Timing windows within a session (New York, London, Asia) where high-probability moves cluster. The Judas swing often prints inside a kill zone. You only need to watch price during them.

SMT Divergence

Disagreement between two correlated assets (BTC vs ETH; SPX vs NASDAQ). One makes a higher high while the other makes a lower high → the divergence flags one print as likely fake.

Risk-to-Reward

R:RBreak-even win rate
1 : 150%
2 : 133%
3 : 125%

Mayne wins roughly high-30s to mid-40s % but is profitable because trades are 2:1 and 3:1. Map entry / stop / target before taking a trade and require ≥ 2:1.

7 · The complete workflow repeatable process

The end-to-end routine, run top-down every time.

  1. HTF bias (Weekly → Daily). Read structure on the weekly (HH/HL or LH/LL?), locate the last MSB, drop to the daily (expect it to look counter-trend during pullbacks). Decide: long or short only.
  2. Dealing range + premium/discount. Draw the significant swing high→low (Fib for 50%). Longs need discount; shorts need premium; never equilibrium.
  3. Liquidity read. What did price just take? Name the next pool (your target — always external range liquidity on the far side). Check the RR is ≥ 2:1. If not, stop here.
  4. Drop to the LTF and wait for the re-sync. Inside the HTF discount/premium zone, watch the counter-trend LTF shift: MSS then MSB in the HTF direction. That's congruence.
  5. Entry model (OB / breaker / FVG). Entry at the 50% mean threshold (OB/breaker) or gap 50% (FVG); stop tight or loose; target the external liquidity from step 3.
  6. Manage and re-run. Don't close early at internal liquidity. Re-establish HTF bias on cadence; restart whenever the HTF structure shifts.

The non-negotiables

  1. Establish HTF bias first; trade top-down.
  2. All 4 checklist boxes (structure / range / liquidity / entry) or no trade.
  3. Longs in discount, shorts in premium, nothing at equilibrium.
  4. A sweep/MSB needs displacement (a close, not just a wick).
  5. IRL = entries, ERL = targets. Don't close early at internal liquidity.
  6. Demand asymmetric RR (≥ 2:1).
  7. Context turns a pattern into a setup — a bare OB/FVG/breaker is just a pattern.

9 · Reading the indicator

The Pine script in this folder (Mayne-SMC-Indicator.pine) mechanically marks the chartable parts of everything above: order blocks, FVGs, bias, the 50%, and BUY/SELL signals. This section ties the theory to what actually prints on your chart.

Which timeframe?

The indicator reads bias and fires off whatever chart you open it on, and can optionally enforce one higher timeframe for you: set Signals → HTF bias timeframe (e.g. 240 while charting M15) and signals that disagree with that HTF's trend are suppressed. Still run it top-down across charts: read the BIAS on the higher one (H12 or Weekly), take signals on the lower (H1 or M15). The gate covers the immediate pair; the read above it — Weekly/Daily — is the judgment call Mayne wants you making yourself.

Zones stop extending once used: the moment price trades into a zone's 50% mean threshold it's "mitigated" and its box freezes at that bar, so only still-actionable zones reach the live bar. Zones also expire from old age (default 500 bars untouched): the box freezes, the zone leaves the table and can no longer signal — old POIs decay, and the fixed bar count makes LTF zones expire faster in wall-clock time than HTF ones.

How a signal fires

A BUY prints only when everything lines up on the same bar: (1) bias is bullish, (2) price is in discount, (3) price retests a bullish OB or FVG at its 50% mean threshold — a genuine retest: the bar that created the zone can't fire it, and a zone whose 50% was already consumed earlier is spent; plus the optional gates: (4) the HTF trend agrees, if HTF bias timeframe is set, and (5) a recent sweep of the range low, if Require liquidity sweep first is on. SELL is the mirror. Signals default to bar-close confirmation (no mid-bar flicker; alerts fire on the close). Each zone fires once; a zone is deleted when price closes through it.

Honest limits — what it does NOT do
  • The HTF gate is one timeframe, not the full stack. It enforces agreement with one higher chart; the Weekly → Daily → H4 read — and spotting an HTF pullback vs a reversal — is still yours. Keep the Weekly open.
  • The sweep filter is mechanical, not the full §2 definition. It checks run + rejection on the current unbroken swing level only — not older equal highs/lows, sweep quality, or the displacement leg.
  • No RR gate, no SMT / kill-zone logic. A signal means "a POI is being retested in the right context" — not "take this trade."
  • Confirms with lag (not a bug). Swings need Swing length bars to confirm, the HTF gate uses the last closed HTF bar, and signals default to bar-close confirmation — late beats repainting.
