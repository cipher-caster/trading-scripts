Reading this guide

A visual companion to the Trader Mayne "Whiteboard Series." Every concept is drawn as schematic candles so you can recognise it on a real chart. The diagram colours match the Pine indicator in this folder, so what you see here is what prints on your TradingView chart.

The five core ideas

  1. Think like the institution. You are the big player who must fill large orders. You can't fill size at market without moving price against yourself, so you engineer liquidity: drive price into resting orders, fill against them, then make the real move.
  2. Price moves from liquidity to liquidity. The market ping-pongs between buy-side (above) and sell-side (below) liquidity. If you can't point at where price is going and why, you don't have a setup.
  3. Top-down, always. Two charts is the shorthand; the method is four jobs — bias, context, setup, entry (§9). The HTF gives bias / structure / points of interest; the LTF gives entries, stops, targets. Never trade off the LTF alone.
  4. Price is fractal (the Russian doll). Inside a weekly range is a daily range, inside that an hourly range. The best trades are where multiple timeframes align.
  5. Markets seek efficiency; asymmetry pays. Inefficient one-directional moves (gaps) get rebalanced; obvious stops get hunted. Trade for asymmetric reward-to-risk so the win rate you need stays low.

1 · Market structure the foundation

Everything is built on this. If you can't read structure cleanly, the rest falls apart. A swing is just three candles; a trend is a sequence of them.

Swing high — middle candle's high is the highest, lower candle each side.
Swing low — middle candle's low is the lowest, higher candle each side.

Mayne uses a 3-candle swing. Swings are visual — the skeleton of price. If a swing isn't obvious, it probably isn't significant.

Uptrend = higher highs (HH) + higher lows (HL).
Downtrend = lower highs (LH) + lower lows (LL).
Range = no clear trend, often equal highs / equal lows. If you can't tell up or down, it's a range. Market structure = trend = direction = bias.

Break vs shift

MSB (continuation) — close through the prior high → assume the uptrend continues.
MSS (potential reversal) — first break against the trend (here, closing back above the last lower high).
Rules — MSB vs MSS
  • MSB = continuation. Uptrend: price closes through the prior higher high. Downtrend: closes through the prior lower low. Requires a close, not just a wick — a wick-through that closes back inside is a liquidity sweep, not a break.
  • MSS = potential reversal. Uptrend: price breaks a higher low. Downtrend: breaks a lower high. "Might" reverse — full reversal is confirmed only when structure then breaks the other way (MSS → LH → MSB down, or MSS → HL → MSB up).

Timeframe congruence — the key skill

A pullback on the HTF (forming a HL) can be a full downtrend on the LTF. The trade is the moment the LTF syncs back up: MSS then MSB in the HTF direction.

Trading the LTF in isolation gets you short right before the HTF reasserts and price rips. Wait for the re-sync.

2 · Liquidity why price moves

For this system, liquidity = resting orders — mostly retail stop losses plus breakout orders. They are the fuel smart money uses to fill positions.

BSL (buy-side liquidity) rests above — short stops + breakout longs; smart money sells into it. SSL rests below — long stops + breakdown shorts; smart money buys it.

Counter-intuition: to get long, price is often driven down first to trigger sells (SSL) the institution buys; to get short, driven up into BSL.

Break-and-close sweep — runs the level, closes back, then displaces.
SFP — a wick through a swing point that closes back inside, then displaces.
Rules — confirming a sweep

Two ingredients are mandatory, or it's a real breakout, not a sweep:

  1. Price runs through the level, then gets rejected (closes back on the original side).
  2. Displacement follows — a strong move, often causing an MSS the other way.

If price breaks and hovers without reclaiming, treat it as a genuine breakout. News (CPI, FOMC, earnings) is just the cover — "it was coming for that liquidity anyway."

ERL (swing high/low) = targets. IRL (minor swings, OBs, FVGs) = entries. Cycle: take ERL → target IRL → take IRL → target ERL.
Equal highs / lows — liquidity rests just beyond. The best-stocked pools on the chart; whether they are your target or your entry is context (below).

A HTF sweep is significant and the move out of it should be powerful. A LTF counter-trend sweep against a strong HTF trend carries less weight — wait for the realign.

Equal highs & equal lows — stronger or weaker? Ep11

The old price-action argument: do repeated touches of a level make it stronger (respected support / resistance) or weaker (each test uses it up)? Mayne's answer is that both sides are wrong — the question itself is retail framing. Multiple touches don't strengthen or weaken anything. All that is happening is liquidity building up at that level, and that liquidity is going to get taken.

Each rejection convinces more traders the level is an iron wall, so they short it and stack stops just above. The third touch does not make it stronger — it makes the pool above it bigger. That is the fuel, and it is why price rips through right when the level looks most reliable.

Read it as an institution: you cannot fill size without an accumulation of resting orders. Where do they accumulate? Above equal highs, below equal lows. A "strong resistance level" is simply the best-stocked pool on the chart.

Identifying them

  • Two or more swings at approximately the same level. Ticks don't matter — 69,420 and 69,415 are equal highs. ICT calls them relatively equal for that reason.
  • Swings, not single candles. Ideally a proper 3-candle swing (§1) that moved away meaningfully, then another one back at the same level.
  • Time and space between them is better. Two swing highs days apart on a HTF chart have had far longer to accumulate orders than two adjacent candles.
  • Double top, triple top, double bottom are the same thing under a different name. Notice how often the third bottom of a "triple bottom" runs the first two — that run is the low.
Engineered liquidity — equal lows left just above your order block. It looks like price is front-running the zone, so you buy early; then it smokes the equal lows into the OB and moves.
The sweep of equal lows leaves the FVG that becomes your entry, and the equal highs left behind become the target. Entry and target from the same cycle.
Rules — trading equal highs / lows
  • They are liquidity, not support or resistance. Above EQH sits buy-side; below EQL sits sell-side. Mark them as pools, never as walls.
  • Entry or target? Context decides — the same context as everything else: HTF bias, dealing range, premium or discount. Bullish range, longing from discount → the EQH in premium is your target. Bearish range → those same EQH in premium are where you look for the reversal.
  • They are not an immediate signal. Clean equal highs say price will eventually go there, not that it goes there now. Timing comes from context.
  • Not always a sweep. Equal highs run in a strong uptrend can simply be a pit stop — the level was a target, and price keeps going. Only context tells you which.
  • The sweep builds the entry. Running EQH/EQL typically leaves an FVG, order block or breaker on the displacement back in — that is what you actually enter on, not the level itself.
  • They are the liquidity in the range framework. EQH/EQL inside the dealing range = internal range liquidity (entries); outside it = external range liquidity (targets). The IRL → ERL → IRL cycle is usually an equal-lows → equal-highs cycle.

Where the accumulation phase of an AMD (§6) leaves equal highs and equal lows on both sides of the range, you get the full picture in one structure: the manipulation runs one side for your entry, the distribution leg runs the other for your target.

3 · Dealing range the working box

At any moment price sits inside a range, heading for liquidity above the high or below the low. The range tells you which is likelier, where to enter, and how far it can go.

Mark the significant swing high → swing low, draw the 50% (equilibrium). Discount below = look for longs. Premium above = look for shorts. "No diddle in the middle" — skip trades at equilibrium.
Rules — building & using the range
  • Mark the significant SH and SL (most recent + visually obvious; discretion allowed).
  • Draw the 50% with the Fib tool (low→high) so 0/50/100 auto-plot.
  • Discount = below 50% → buy low. Premium = above 50% → sell high. Equilibrium = no trade.
  • BSL sits above the high (external), SSL below the low (external).
  • Bullish range = swing low first then high; bearish = swing high first then low.
  • The range resets every MSB — each new leg becomes a new dealing range; redraw.
Nested ranges (Russian doll). Highest-probability long = hourly and daily and weekly all in discount. Zooming into the LTF range gives a far tighter stop and better RR than entering off the HTF.

IRL = entries, ERL = targets. Don't close at internal liquidity still inside the range, then watch price run to the real external target without you.

4 · High timeframe bias the master filter

The single most important filter — it answers one question: long or short? Establish it before looking for any entry. The highest timeframe is your boss; whatever it says goes.

If weekly + daily are bearish, the best M15 trade is a short, even amid a M15 uptrend. Bias proliferates top-down.

Don't jump Weekly straight to an hourly entry — step down through the hierarchy.

The 4-point checklist — run before EVERY trade

  1. HTF market structure — bull or bear? Where was the last MSB? What are weekly + daily doing?
  2. Dealing range — premium, discount, or equilibrium? (Longs only in discount; shorts only in premium; nothing at EQ.)
  3. Liquidity — what did we just take? What's the next pool, and does the RR to it make sense (asymmetric, ≥ 2:1)?
  4. Entry model — is there a clean POI (order block, breaker, or FVG)?

All four boxes or no trade. One fail (e.g. you're in premium not discount) = skip it, even if it would have worked. Re-establish bias on cadence — whenever the HTF shifts, start over.

5 · Entries: order blocks, breakers, FVGs points of interest

The actual entry zones. All three live in the same areas and the best setups overlap them. Context from §§1–4 is what separates a high- from low-probability POI — a POI in isolation is just a pattern.

Fair Value Gap (FVG)

Bullish FVG — gap from high of candle 1 to low of candle 3, left by a big green displacement candle. Price returns to fill it.
Bearish FVG — gap from low of candle 1 to high of candle 3, left by a big red displacement candle.
Rules — trading an FVG
  • Entry: the gap's 50% and into the zone — bullish: upper 50% (or the top edge); bearish: lower 50%. Ladder ("spray") across that half.
  • Stop: tight = just beyond the far edge of the gap (best RR); loose = beyond candle 1 of the pattern (fewer stop-outs, worse RR).
  • Target: external range liquidity on the opposite side.
  • Higher-probability when: in the correct half, formed after sweeping liquidity, aligned with HTF trend, bigger displacement candle. Not all FVGs fill, and not all that fill hold.

Order Block (OB)

Bullish OB — the last down candle before a strong up-move (ideally one that breaks structure). Demand zone; look for longs on retest.
Bearish OB — the last up candle before a strong down-move. Supply zone; look for shorts on retest.
Rules — trading an OB
  • Entry: from the open/top of the OB down to its 50% (mean threshold) for a bullish OB; bottom up to 50% for bearish. A strong OB should not close beyond its 50%.
  • Stop: just beyond the full OB (+ room for spread). Safer because a good OB already ran liquidity.
  • Target: external range liquidity (BSL above for longs, SSL below for shorts).
  • Higher-probability when: large displacement off it (leaves an FVG), the move breaks structure, it took liquidity first, bigger candle(s). OBs trade with the trend.

Breaker & the "unicorn" overlap

Breaker = a failed OB, i.e. an S/R flip. A failed bearish OB that price blows through becomes support → longs. Most common at reversals.
When OB + FVG (+ breaker) stack, that's the highest-probability POI in the system. Alarm bells — full attention.

OB vs FVG — which do you use? Ep8

A good OB creates an FVG, so they sit in the same area (OB just below, FVG just above in a bull leg). The recurring question: will price reverse at the FVG, or trade down to the OB? Mayne's honest answer is "it depends" — run five filters, and the last is the real decider.

  1. Location (premium/discount) — first and most important. Only POIs on the correct side; throws out most candidates.
  2. Context — aggressive trend → favour FVGs (shallow pullbacks); range → favour OBs; reversal → OBs + breakers.
  3. Quality — prefer the cleaner, more obvious POI. Tiny ambiguous candle = probably not the one.
  4. Risk-to-reward — if the FVG doesn't give ≥ 2R, wait for the deeper OB or accept you may miss the trade.
  5. Low timeframe — the actual answer. Zoom in. Whichever POI is where the LTF flips from downtrend to uptrend (MSS → MSB, re-syncing with the HTF) is the one that holds. If the OB also fails to flip, that's a likely trend change — stand aside.

Mayne's own practice: HTF POIs = OBs and breakers; LTF entries = breakers mainly. FVGs shine on micro timeframes (15m → 5m) in a clean trend. The real rule is use what works for you; obviousness is the tell.

6 · Power of three AMD · the Judas swing

Before price makes a move it fakes the other way first. That fake-out is not noise, it is the mechanism, and it repeats on every chart and every timeframe: accumulation → manipulation → distribution. Name the phase you are in and the fake-out stops costing you money and starts handing you entries.

The three phases

Bullish AMD — price ranges (A), breaks the range low to trigger sell stops (M), then displaces back inside and distributes up (D).
Bearish AMD — same machine inverted: the manipulation runs above the range into buy stops, then price distributes down.

Manipulation is the sweep / SFP from §2 wearing a different name. ICT calls it the Judas swing — the move that betrays the direction price actually wants to go. In a range, liquidity is building on both sides; the manipulation picks the side it wants to fill against.

Rules — reading AMD live
  • A — accumulation. Sideways. No clear direction, by definition (that is what a range is). Buy stops pile above it, sell stops below it.
  • M — manipulation. A break of the accumulation range that runs one of those pools. To get long, price must go down to find sellers; to get short, up to find buyers.
  • Displacement is the confirmation — a strong close back inside the accumulation range, often leaving an FVG. No displacement = treat it as a genuine break, not a Judas swing.
  • D — distribution. The real leg, in the intended direction. This is where the money is made; A and M only tell you where to get on.
  • Which side breaks? Not the range — the HTF bias (§4). Bullish HTF: a breakdown that gets reclaimed is manipulation, expect the AMD to resolve up. Bearish HTF: a breakout that gets reclaimed is manipulation, expect it to resolve down.
  • Invalidation is losing the level you reclaimed, or trading back through the manipulation extreme.

Every candle is a power of three

Every candle has an open, a high, a low and a close. It always starts at the open and ends at the close, but the high and low swap places: a clean bullish candle runs open → low → high → close, a clean bearish one open → high → low → close. The open is accumulation, the wick is manipulation, the body is distribution.

Practical consequence: enter in the wick, hold through the body, exit near the close. The wicks are where you get filled, not where you get trapped. Not every candle prints in that order — a bearish candle can make its low first — but the ones that give you a clean power of three usually do.

The four schematics worth learning Ep10

ICT-style AMD gets elaborate (standard deviations, precise times of day). Mayne's position: that is probably overkill. Four schematics carry the concept. Each one has an anchor — the open you measure the manipulation against.

Same logic, different clock. Bearish week: price trades above the weekly open early, gets back below it Tue/Wed, and the high of the week is in. The single-candle AMD is identical again but has no session rules of thumb — the only anchor is that candle's own open, so mark it on the LTF and watch for the break-and-reclaim inside the candle's life.

The visual AMD does not have to fit inside one candle of any timeframe. You read it straight off the chart: accumulate, fake out, go. It layers cleanly with fibs — the displacement leg gives you the dealing range, and the pullback into the OTE / FVG is the entry.

Layering it onto the framework

AMD is not a separate system — it is a timing layer on top of §§1–5. HTF bias says long. The manipulation leg sweeps sell-side liquidity into a HTF order block in discount. Displacement back inside confirms the Judas swing, and the FVG it leaves is the entry on the distribution leg.
Rules — trading an AMD
  1. Bias first. Decide whether the next weekly / daily / H4 candle should be green or red using the HTF read (§4). That decides which way the AMD resolves. Without it you are guessing.
  2. Mark the anchor — weekly, daily or that candle's open — and the accumulation range around it.
  3. Wait for the break of the range in the direction against your bias. That is the candidate manipulation, and it is the discount (or premium) you wanted.
  4. Demand displacement back through the anchor. Reclaiming the open with a strong close is confirmation. Enter on the FVG or order block that displacement leaves.
  5. Stack it. Manipulation into an OB, at the OTE, sweeping equal lows, inside the HTF discount — the more of the framework that lines up on the same level, the better the trade. AMD adds one more layer, it does not replace any of them.
  6. Target the distribution leg — external range liquidity on the far side, held through the body of the candle, not scalped at the first internal pool.

Don't force it. The rules of thumb are rules of thumb: a bullish week can print its high on Monday. If the schematic isn't there — the high of the week formed early while you're bullish, or the Asian range is a mess — that week or that day is simply not an AMD trade. Take the setup another way or don't take it.

7 · Frameworks

The supporting concepts that sit around the core system. AMD and the Judas swing have their own section (§6); these are the rest.

Kill Zones

Timing windows within a session (New York, London, Asia) where high-probability moves cluster. The Judas swing often prints inside a kill zone. You only need to watch price during them.

SMT Divergence

Disagreement between two correlated assets (BTC vs ETH; SPX vs NASDAQ). One makes a higher high while the other makes a lower high → the divergence flags one print as likely fake.

Risk-to-Reward

8 · The complete workflow repeatable process

The end-to-end routine, run top-down every time.

  1. HTF bias (Weekly → Daily). Read structure on the weekly (HH/HL or LH/LL?), locate the last MSB, drop to the daily (expect it to look counter-trend during pullbacks). Decide: long or short only.
  2. Dealing range + premium/discount. Draw the significant swing high→low (Fib for 50%). Longs need discount; shorts need premium; never equilibrium.
  3. Liquidity read. What did price just take? Name the next pool (your target — always external range liquidity on the far side). Check the RR is ≥ 2:1. If not, stop here.
  4. Step down for the re-sync — H4/H1 first. Inside the HTF discount/premium zone, the shift must be visible on the H4 or H1 (MSB + FVG, OB, breaker or SFP) before you open the execution chart: MSS then MSB in the HTF direction. That's congruence. Going from HTF bias straight to the 5m is the single most common misread of this process (§9).
  5. Entry model (OB / breaker / FVG). Entry at the 50% mean threshold (OB/breaker) or gap 50% (FVG); target the external liquidity from step 3. Stop by trade type, not by geometry: reversal → beyond the H4/H1 zone (expect several LTF attempts to fail inside it); continuation → just beyond the LTF swing (§10).
  6. Manage and re-run. Don't close early at internal liquidity. Re-establish HTF bias on cadence; restart whenever the HTF structure shifts.

The four-step process Ep12

Same routine, formalised. The low timeframe is for execution, not decisions — every decision is already made by the time you open it. Four steps, always in this order, one job each.

StepTimeframeJobWhat you leave with
1Weekly → DailyBias & directionLong-only or short-only, plus the HTF ranges, OBs and liquidity marked on the chart
2H4ContextWhere you are in the daily range: premium/discount, OTE, equal highs/lows, AMD, the H4 POI
3H1The setupNothing — until price actually trades into that POI

The timeframes are interchangeable as long as they stay evenly spaced and you run all four: Daily → H4 → H1 → M15 → M1 works, so does H4 → H1/M30 → M15/M5 → M1. Skipping a step is what breaks it, not the choice of charts. The six steps above map onto these: workflow 1–2 = step 1, workflow 3 = step 2, workflow 4 = step 3, workflow 5 = step 4.

Step 1, second half — weekly bullish but the daily still prints LH/LL into HTF demand. The pullback isn't finished. No longs, no shorts; wait.
The daily shifts inside that zone — the pullback is done and the daily has re-synced with the weekly. Now step 2 starts.

Weekly ranging = no bias = no trade. Mark the weekly and daily ranges, OBs and liquidity in different colours as you go, so that on the execution chart you don't short straight into a weekly demand zone you'd forgotten was there.

Step 2 — mark the map, take nothing. Equilibrium, OTE, the equal highs you'll target, the H4 POI you'll trade. "Look at the map before you enter the maze."
Step 3 — the H1's only job is waiting for price to trade into that POI. Hours or days. Set alerts. No tag, no trade.
Rules — running the four steps
  • Step 1 (W/D): weekly structure gives direction — broke a high = bullish, broke a low = bearish, ranging = stand down. Then read the daily inside that: daily downtrend under a bullish weekly = pullback still in progress, expect it to go lower and mitigate something first; daily MSS/uptrend = pullback complete, the higher low is being put in.
  • Step 2 (H4): repeat the second half of step 1 one level down — where are we in the daily range? Premium/discount, equilibrium, OTE, order blocks, the OB that caused the MSB, equal highs/lows, AMD, fair value gaps. A few minutes' work. You are not entering here.
  • Step 3 (H1): do nothing but wait for price to reach one of those marked levels. The session AMD is also visible here. Most people fail by dropping to the M5 at this point, seeing something that looks like a setup and getting smoked.
  • Step 4 (M5): inside the H4 zone, look for an MSB in the HTF direction with displacement — that leaves an FVG, and almost always an OB and breaker with it. Entry on the pullback into it.
  • Stop from the HTF, target from the HTF. The LTF buys you a pinpoint entry and nothing else. Invalidation is the H4 POI, not the M5 low — one MSB inside a big HTF zone can fail and the second attempt work. Targets are the H4 equal highs, the daily range high, then the weekly range high.
  • Each step contributes one piece. You cannot take a trade off one of them alone.

If the stop below the H4 POI looks like it ruins the RR, you are targeting the wrong thing — the target is external HTF liquidity, not the nearest LTF swing.

9 · Entry models step 4 · execution

Two entry models and a time-based variant. They are the same pattern on different charts, and they all live in step 4 of the four-step process — they do not work in a vacuum. If steps 1–3 aren't done, none of this is a setup.

The 5-minute model Ep13

Five steps. All of them, in this order, or you don't have the trade. In six words: zone · structure · displacement · FVG · stop · target.

(1) price trades into the HTF zone → (2) M5 MSB in the HTF direction → (3) displacement → (4) the gap it leaves is the entry, taken on the pullback → (5) stop under the M5 low, target from the HTF.
  1. Zone tag. Price must trade into the HTF POI — not near it. Could take hours or days; it may never come. Then there's no trade.
  2. M5 market structure break in the direction you want. Bullish bias: the M5 is in a downtrend inside your zone, you want its last lower high taken. Bearish: the last higher low. This doubles as a filter on the zone itself — if price just keeps making LL/LH inside it, the level isn't being respected.
  3. Displacement. The break must come with a displacement candle. It does three things: confirms the break isn't a fake-out, creates the FVG, and tells you institutional sponsorship is on your side.
  4. Pull back to the FVG and enter. The gap is the entry, not the break. Don't chase the displacement candle — price nearly always returns. Market order, limit order, or wait for a reaction at the gap: personal preference.
  5. Stop and target. Stop above/below the M5 swing that preceded the break, or beyond the HTF zone. Target is HTF liquidity, minimum 2:1.

The FVG alone is enough for this model. An OB or breaker stacked on it is better (§5's unicorn), but not required. Displacement can be one candle or several — two adjacent FVGs on the break-leg count.

When it doesn't work

No zone tag. Price stalls above it and turns. It doesn't matter how clean the M5 break looks — no tag, no setup.
Break without displacement — overlapping candles, no gap left behind, nothing to enter on. Occasionally still works; far lower odds.

Third failure mode: the setup exists but doesn't offer 2:1 — typical when you're taking a continuation leg already close to the target. Skip it. You will watch skipped trades work; that's the filter doing its job.

The 15-minute model Ep14

Identical model, one chart up. Break of structure in the HTF direction, FVG, entry on the pullback, stop below the M15 low or beyond the H4/H1 zone. Nothing new to learn — the only thing that changes is the trade-off.

M5 inside one HTF zone: several textbook MSB+FVG setups fail before the real turn. Take every one and it's death by a thousand cuts.
The same stretch on M15 — the failed triggers become wicks. One break, one FVG, one entry. Later and higher, but you weren't in the two that failed.

Mayne's publicly-posted "H12 / H1" setups are this process compressed, not a different system: H12 ≈ daily (two H12 candles = one daily), and the weekly and H4 reads are still happening, just not shown. The pairs line up like this:

The clarification: you can't time a reversal off the M5 alone Ep14

A viewer's comment on Ep13 — "you're not going to time the daily reversal on the five-minute chart" — is correct as stated, and Mayne's answer refines how both models are applied. The missing step was between HTF bias and the model: you need the H4 or H1 to show the turn first — an MSB with an FVG, an order block, a breaker, or an SFP. Only then do you drop to M15/M5 and time it. You never go from HTF bias straight to the execution chart.

The same clarification splits the stop rule in two, and the two setup types with it:

Reversal — you're timing the HTF turn, so expect several failed LTF attempts inside the zone. Your invalidation is the H4/H1 POI, not the M5 low. Wider stop, smaller size, best entry when it works.
Continuation — the HTF has already shifted, so the LTF low is a real invalidation. Stop tucks under it. Tighter, higher probability, worse entry.

Once the daily has shifted and you're heading for external liquidity, every H4/H1 OB and FVG on the way up is a continuation setup — run the same model inside each one.

The Silver Bullet time-based

An ICT model: the same sequence, gated by the clock. Inside a one-hour window, price sweeps liquidity, breaks structure, creates a fair value gap, then trades the other way — which is the entry model plus a session-scale AMD (Asian accumulation → session-open manipulation → distribution through the rest of the day).

Sweep → MSS with displacement → FVG → entry on the retest, all inside the window. Nothing new; the clock is the extra filter.

All three are quoted New York time — the transcript's words: "These are all New York time. Okay, so this is Eastern Standard Time." (Mayne's closing recap misstates them as "8 to 11:00 a.m. New York time"; the three windows given in the body of the episode are the ones above.) The premise is that institutional algorithms are more active then; Mayne's own position on that premise is "if that sounds like a bunch of mumbo jumbo BS, I have no comment" — but the times line up with real volume, and his own testing shows a 5-minute model that already agrees with the HTF has a higher hit rate inside the window.

Rules — the counter-trend exception

  5. Entries are LTF; targets are HTF. Take partials at internal liquidity if you like, but don't close the position at the nearest M5 swing.

10 · Stops, targets & RR risk first

You can have the perfect entry and still lose money — the entry is one third of the trade. This section is the other two thirds plus the maths that lets you lose more than half your trades and still make money.

The stop goes where the idea dies

One line: your stop equals your invalidation. Not a fixed number of points, not under a round number, not wherever gives you the reward:risk you wanted. Ask what validated the setup — HTF bias, HTF point of interest, the LTF model (MSS/MSB + displacement + FVG). The stop sits beyond whatever price must break to make all of that wrong.

Reversal model: entry inside the HTF POI, stop beyond the whole POI. Price can trade deeper into the zone and still reverse — you are not wrong until it closes through the far edge.

Two placements, same logic. Reversal — stop past the far edge of the HTF POI. Continuation — stop past the swing low/high that produced the structure break you entered on. Lose that structure, lose the trade.

Don't park it where everyone else's is

Stop tucked under the obvious equal lows — that pool is exactly what gets hunted. You get taken out on the sweep, then price rips without you.
Same candles, stop past the zone's far edge with breathing room for wicks, spread and slippage. The sweep is the reason you're long, not your invalidation.

Never put the stop on the exact low tick of the order block. A dollar of overshoot for one second should not end the trade. Build that buffer in before you size the position — it is part of the entry-minus-stop distance. This is also why Mayne trades SFPs: the sweep has already happened, so the obvious liquidity below is spent.

Rules — the three stop behaviours
  1. Hard order, placed immediately. Not a mental stop. The moment the entry goes on, the stop order goes on the platform with it. A mental stop is a negotiation you lose — you hold "a little longer", you fall asleep, the loss doubles.
  2. Never widen it against you. Not once, not ever. Widening blows up the position-size maths that the original stop generated: the $2,000 you agreed to lose quietly becomes $3,000. Trailing a stop up with the trade is fine; moving it away never is.
  3. Position size comes from the stop. Leverage and margin are not in the equation at all. Know the entry, know the stop, know the risk — the size falls out.

size = (risk % × account balance) ÷ (entry − stop)

Risk 0.5–2% per trade, and keep it static — think in percent, not dollars, so 2% still feels like 2% when the account is a million. Five open trades at 2% is 10% exposed, so count correlated positions. Your platform's order window does the arithmetic; you just need to understand where the number comes from.

Risk-first framing. Before entry, state the worst case out loud: "stopped out costs me $2,000." Being $1,000 in drawdown is then a non-event — you already accepted twice that. Most people think reward first and never price the loss.

Targets — internal to external

Targets are not a judgement call; they are the other half of the liquidity cycle from §2. Enter at internal range liquidity → target external. Enter off an external sweep → target internal. Where you entered tells you where you're going.

Long from an OB inside the range (IRL) → the target is the external range high. Internal liquidity on the way — equal highs, a swing high — is where partials come off, not where the trade ends.
Rules — partials, and the two ways people ruin them
  • Default: assume the whole thing closes at 2:1. That is how Mayne reports his trades unless he says otherwise.
  • If you take partials: increments of 25% or 1/3. Peel 25–33% at obvious internal liquidity on the way up; by the time price reaches 2R you are 50–75% (or 2/3) out; the last 25–33% is the moon bag riding to the external target.
  • TP-ing too early is a position-size symptom. If a green number makes you want to close immediately, you're too big — the same tell as closing red trades before the stop.
  • Break-even too early donates the position back. Your entry was an entry; why would it be a good stop? Only move the stop once price has cleared a meaningful level and you've taken something off — then trail to the new structure low.
  • Early on, don't do any of this. Set entry, stop and target at the start and don't touch them. Take the whole trade off at 2:1 or 3:1. Mechanical beats clever until you're consistent.

The receipt: a 100K account, a static 2% risk, trades closed primarily at 2R — more than doubled in a little over a year.

Reward:risk — the whole point

Reward:risk is entry→target divided by entry→stop. Map all three before you click. Minimum accepted: 2:1.

The most common self-deception: the honest target gives 1:1, so the target gets dragged to a level nothing is resting at. You don't move the target to manufacture RR — and you don't tighten the stop to do it either. The setup either offers 2:1 or it isn't a trade.

Stop comes from the chart. Target comes from the chart. If what the chart gives you isn't 2:1, that trade was already being filtered out — see §12.

11 · Scaling in building the position

Everything so far has been all-in at one price: price hits the zone, you fill full size. That works, but your points of interest are zones, not lines. Scaling means taking the position size you already calculated and breaking it into pieces across that zone. There is a razor thin line between scaling into a good trade and averaging down a bad one.

What scaling is — and isn't

Scaling: risk 1% (= 1R = $1,000 on a 100K account). The maths from §10 says that's 1 BTC. You enter 0.3 + 0.3 + 0.4, or 0.25 × 4. Total exposure: still 1 BTC, still 1%. You broke up the entry, not the risk.

Not scaling: long 1 BTC, it moves, long another 1 BTC, it dips, long a third. That's a 3 BTC position and 3% of risk on a setup that priced out at 1%. Nothing about the position size was recalculated — it was just tripled.

None of the numbers are unknown. Risk %, account size, entry, stop — the size is already determined before the first fill. Scaling only decides how that fixed size arrives.

Method 1 — ladder the zone

All-in at the top edge. You get filled on any touch, but if price works through the zone your entry is the worst price in it and the stop is the full zone away.
Same candles, three fills across the zone. Shallow pullback: you still have exposure. Deep pullback: the average drops toward the far edge, so the same 1R now sits closer to invalidation — better entry and better RR.

You have no idea how deep into a daily or H4 order block price will trade. It might turn a third of the way in, or fill the gap and eat half the OB first. Laddering means you get some exposure either way. Stop trying to bottom-tick — the money is in the distribution after the reversal, not in the last dollar of the wick.

Rules — laddering a zone
  • Thirds is the default (fourths if you prefer). Mayne uses thirds.
  • At least one fill at the top of every PD array. If an FVG sits on top of an OB: one at the top of the FVG, one at the bottom of the FVG, one in the middle of the OB. Where they overlap is the highest-probability pocket, so weight it.
  • The stop does not move. You split the entry, not the stop — one stop, sitting where the idea is invalidated, covering the whole 1 BTC. That's what keeps risk at 1%.
  • Don't put the entry on a single line. Pinning the whole position to the mean threshold to the tick means you either miss the fill entirely or watch price trade straight through it.

Method 2 — add on confirmation

First entry when the LTF model prints inside the HTF zone — the riskiest fill, so it's the smallest. Each add requires its own complete model: a new MSB and a new FVG. Full size is on only after the trade has proved itself.

Every new model is also a new invalidation, so the stop can come up to each new model low. You end up at full size with a far tighter stop than you started with. Note the trade-off against method 1: laddering a zone drives the average down, adding on confirmation drives it up — you pay a worse average for proof.

An MSB with no fair value gap is not an add. Price breaking structure and running is not an add. Wait for the break, the displacement, and the pullback into the gap — every single time.

Laddering both together

0.5 BTC on the touch of the daily OB (so a reversal that never prints a LTF model still gets you in), then 0.25 + 0.25 on M15 models as they confirm. Still 1 BTC, still 1R — covered for a shallow bounce, a deep fill, and a straight-line reversal.

The failure case this protects against: the daily zone breaks, the LTF never turns, no model ever prints — and you're down 0.5R instead of a full 1R, because the confirming half never got added. That's the whole argument for scaling in one sentence.

Averaging down — the thing that looks identical and isn't

Price closes through the zone — that was invalidation — and the adds keep coming because "it's cheaper now." A planned $1,000 loss becomes $1,500, then $2,000.

Two tells, and they are the only two:

  1. You're past invalidation. The trade idea is already dead; anything you add is risk you never budgeted. If the hard stop order from §10 were actually on the platform, you would have been taken out and this could not happen.
  2. There's no model. You zoom in and the LTF is making lower lows and lower highs — no MSB, no FVG, no sign of life — and you buy anyway. The zone is telling you it won't hold.

The difference is planning. Scaling is decided before the first fill: this much here, this much there, this much on confirmation, one stop, total risk 1R. Averaging down is improvised — negotiating with the invalidation, hoping the level was slightly wrong. If it feels like you're making it up as you go, you're averaging down a loser.

And the honest caveat: one entry, one stop, don't touch it is still the right answer until the basic model is second nature. Scaling and trailing stops are what you graduate to, not what you start with.

What the indicator does and doesn't do here

Very little of this is mechanised, and the guide should be honest about it. A zone fires once, as a single BUY/SELL label — the script has no concept of a partial fill, an average entry, or an add-on. The manual hook is the zone table: the Entry (range) column is the full bottom–top of the zone precisely so you can ladder your orders across it, with the 50% column as the middle rung. Trigger at zone 50% off = the signal fires at first touch of the edge, which is the top rung of a ladder rather than the whole position.

The RR the label prints assumes a single fill. Ladder the zone and your real average sits below it, so your actual R is better than the number shown — the readout is conservative, not wrong. Stop buffer beyond far edge (×ATR) still describes the one stop that covers all your rungs, which is the correct model. Nothing in the script detects averaging down; only your own hard stop does.

12 · When not to trade the filter

Every other section is about what to look for. This one is the inverse, and it may be the most important: your edge is a filter, and what separates a profitable trader from an average one is the ability to throw trades away. Risk and attention are both finite — most of what you see on a chart or a timeline deserves neither.

Seven disqualifiers. If any one of them is true, there is no trade. They are deliberately binary — you should be able to answer each in a second, without argument.

#DisqualifierThe question you ask
1No HTF biasCan I say bullish or bearish immediately? A "maybe" is a no.
2Wrong zoneLong but not in discount? Short but not in premium? Out.
3No liquidity target, or the maths failsWhere is it going, and does it pay 2:1?
4Model didn't fully triggerDid every step happen, in order?
5You're chasingDid I get my fill where I said I would, or am I paying up?
6NewsCPI / FOMC / NFP / earnings today? Sit out.
7You're emotionalAngry, tired, on tilt, FOMO, revenge? Close the laptop.

1 · No high timeframe bias

If you can't tell within seconds whether the weekly / daily is up or down, the deck isn't stacked. A ranging HTF means longs can work and shorts can work — that's a coin flip, not an edge.

Rated by Mayne as the most important and the easiest of the seven. It is also the §4 master filter restated as a veto instead of a green light.

2 · Price is in the wrong zone

Bias is bullish, price is in premium. A long here isn't impossible, it's just less probable — and low-probability is exactly what the system exists to reject.

The alternative isn't "take it anyway", it's wait: sit out, or let price come to your side of the 50%. That's patience, not inaction.

3 · No clear liquidity target, or the maths doesn't work

Stop 10 points away, the only real liquidity 10 points above: a 1:1. The maths does not work, so there is no trade — regardless of how good the entry looks. Where the trade is going matters as much as where the stop is.

Two separate failures live in this one item. (a) Price is mid-range, the highs and lows are already taken, nothing obvious is being targeted — no trade. (b) There is a target but it pays less than 2:1 — no trade. And per §10, you fix neither by dragging the target further out.

4 · The model didn't fully trigger

Price reached the HTF zone and structure broke — but no displacement, no fair value gap, so there is nothing to retrace into. Entering here is skipping a step.

The keyword is fully. Zone → HTF clue (is H1/H4 bullish now?) → MSB → displacement leaving an FVG → retrace into the FVG for the entry. In that order, every time. Front-running any one of them — "there hasn't been a break yet but I'm confident there will be" — is a disqualifier, not a judgement call.

5 · You're chasing

The displacement leaves your gap and never comes back to fill you. That's a missed trade, not a trade. We do business where we decided to do business.

Both flavours count: getting to the screen after the move and buying anyway, and price coming "close enough" to the zone without tagging it while you front-run your own level. The predictable ending — you buy the extension, it retraces, you close red, and then it goes.

6 · News

Around CPI, FOMC, NFP or earnings you routinely get both sides of the range run before the real move. The event is the cover story for taking liquidity from everyone gambling on the headline.

The NVIDIA example: beat earnings, pumped, everyone bought — then the market sold off, ran the stops, trapped the longs, and only then made the actual up-move. Trading before, during, or right after the print is gambling. Sit out, let the dust settle, let price show you how it absorbed the news, then look for a setup.

7 · You're emotional

No diagram — this one isn't on the chart. Anger, fear, greed, FOMO, tilt, revenge after two losses, a bad night's sleep, a fight before you sat down. Rated as big a filter as #1. Emotion is what makes you skip the other six items, and you'll only notice afterwards, the same way you notice something you said in an argument once you've cooled off.

The meta-rule

All seven are meant to be clear, obvious and immediate — the same standard as spotting a fair value gap or a sweep. If a disqualifier needs a long argument with yourself to dismiss, that's the tell: it applies. When in doubt, don't. And review the trades you filtered out, not just the ones you took — that's where the edge actually shows up.

Honest limits — what it does NOT do
  • The HTF gate is one timeframe, not the full stack. It enforces agreement with one higher chart; the Weekly → Daily → H4 read — and spotting an HTF pullback vs a reversal — is still yours. Keep the Weekly open.
  • The sweep filter is mechanical, not the full §2 definition. It checks run + rejection on the current unbroken swing level only — not older equal highs/lows, sweep quality, or the displacement leg.
  • No SMT / kill-zone logic. The RR readout/gate uses mechanical stops and targets (zone edge ± ATR, range extreme / next zone) — sane defaults, not Mayne's read of an individual setup. A signal means "a POI is being retested in the right context" — not "take this trade."
  • Standard charts only. Heikin Ashi / Renko / Kagi / P&F / Range charts are rejected — their synthetic OHLC would fabricate structure and zones.
  • Confirms with lag (not a bug). Swings need Swing length bars to confirm, the HTF gate uses the last closed HTF bar, and signals default to bar-close confirmation — late beats repainting.
